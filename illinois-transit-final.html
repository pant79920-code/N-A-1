Why Public Transit

Transit systems move people without the per-capita emissions, land use, and congestion cost of single-occupancy driving. Illinois runs six meaningfully different systems side by side: big-city rail and bus (CTA), suburban commuter rail (Metra), suburban bus (Pace), statewide intercity rail (Amtrak Illinois), and a small downstate city bus system (SMTD, Springfield). Together they cover almost every transit archetype in the state — which also means comparing them exposes real gaps: SMTD's funding and service levels are a fraction of what Chicago-area riders get, despite serving a real working population downstate.

In December 2025, Illinois passed the Northern Illinois Transit Authority (NITA) Act, replacing the RTA and adding roughly $1.2 billion in new annual operating funding plus $180 million in capital funding for CTA, Metra, and Pace — averting a widely-discussed "fiscal cliff" that threatened service cuts and fare hikes across the region.

This page is an advocacy resource, not a neutral policy review — the framing has a point of view. But every number on this page has been checked against agency and RTA-published sources where possible, and estimates that couldn't be verified are labeled Estimate rather than presented as fact. Two calculations in the original working notes for this project (household savings and congestion-cost-avoided, computed by multiplying full annual driving costs or per-driver congestion costs by trip counts instead of per-mile rates by trip distance) produced results in the hundreds of billions of dollars — larger than Illinois' entire state budget. Both have been recalculated below using a unit-consistent, sourced per-mile method; see the methodology note at the bottom of the page for exactly what changed.

Current Illinois Transit Policy

  • Northern Illinois Transit Authority (NITA) Act — reorganizes regional transit governance, replacing the RTA, signed December 2025.
  • ~$1.2B in new annual operating funding — supports CTA, Metra, and Pace; averted the widely-reported 2026 "fiscal cliff" that would have forced service cuts and fare hikes.
  • 0.25% regional sales-tax increase — new dedicated revenue source for transit operations.
  • State gasoline sales-tax redirection — 85% of the state portion of the gas sales tax now directed to the NITA region.
  • Transit safety & security funding — additional funding for security personnel on buses and trains.
  • Accessibility investment — CTA and Metra station ADA improvements, including elevators.
  • Zero-emission bus investment — electrifying CTA and Pace fleets and facilities.
  • Red Line Extension — major CTA capital project to extend Red Line service.
  • Access to Transit program — sidewalk and bike-connection funding linking neighborhoods to stations.
  • "Next Move Illinois" — IDOT's statewide transit strategy, explicitly including downstate systems like SMTD.

The Case for Public Transit

Public transit and urbanism have become a lower priority in America than in comparable countries, in part because of how heavily car-centric infrastructure has been favored for decades. That has left most of the U.S. — outside a handful of major cities — with few real transportation alternatives. But by the numbers on this page, public transit is more economically efficient for working-class riders than car ownership, and it materially reduces the emissions driving climate change. Investing in it isn't just an environmental position — it's a direct financial benefit to the people who ride it, and a shared benefit (through reduced congestion and pollution) to everyone else in the state, including people who never set foot on a train or bus.

Methodology & Honesty Notes

What was corrected before publishing: Two calculation patterns in the working notes for this project produced results that were off by orders of magnitude — in some cases larger than Illinois' entire state GDP. (1) "Household savings" was originally computed as trip-count × full annual car-ownership cost, rather than trip-count × trip-distance × per-mile driving cost — fixed using AAA's ~$0.70/mile marginal driving cost. (2) "Congestion cost avoided" used the same flawed pattern (annual per-driver cost × total trip count) and produced numbers like $98.6 billion for CTA Rail alone — more than 10x Illinois' actual statewide congestion cost. It's now restored using a unit-consistent method: miles avoided × a real per-mile cost derived from INRIX's 2025 Chicago Global Traffic Scorecard (112 hours and $2,063 lost per average Chicago driver per year). Pace's ridership vs. "cars removed" claim also contained an internal contradiction (claimed avoided trips exceeded total ridership) and was recalculated from Pace's actual ridership.

What's still an estimate, clearly labeled: All CO₂/SO₂/NOₓ figures depend on car-replacement percentages drawn from rider surveys that are, in most cases, agency-wide rather than mode-specific (flagged inline where that's true). CTA's rail/bus operating-cost split is not a CTA-published figure — it's an estimate applied to CTA's real combined budget, and is labeled as such throughout. The congestion figures for Amtrak and SMTD reuse Chicago's INRIX rate as the only available benchmark, which almost certainly overstates their real congestion-relief value since neither system operates primarily in dense urban traffic — both are labeled "Est., weak" for that reason.
